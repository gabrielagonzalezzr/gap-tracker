Digital finance in Latin America: owning an account
versus actually using one

A second case study tracking the gap between research on women's financial inclusion and what's actually happening across the region, focused on digital and mobile money access.

Theory
Owning a phone or an account isn't the same as using digital financial tools. The real barrier is confidence with complex tasks — transfers, apps — not just basic ones.
Practice
Regional account ownership grew from 39% to 75% since 2011, and mobile money accounts grew 15 points since 2021 alone. Access is expanding fast.
The gap
The gender gap in account ownership still sits at 8 points regionally, reaching 20 points in El Salvador. In Mexico, 81% of women own a phone but only half have a bank account.

Mechanism

Research on women's digital capability consistently finds a much wider gender gap for complex phone tasks (transfers, apps, downloads) than for basic ones (calls, texts) — a pattern found across multiple developing-economy studies, not unique to Latin America. The barrier isn't lack of an account. It's confidence and trust in the technology itself, compounded by connectivity gaps and, in some countries, social norms about who manages household money. That reframes the fix from "open more accounts" to "build trust and remove the need for digital confidence in the first place."

Two structural fixes, same target
Agent-assisted onboarding
Physical cash-in/cash-out points, like Mercado Pago's 20,000-plus partner stores in Mexico, let people build trust in digital accounts through a human intermediary.
Default digital enrollment
Government transfers paid directly into digital wallets, like Argentina's ANSES program with Mercado Pago, remove the need for upfront financial literacy entirely.
