Civil legal aid: a right that only
exists for some cases

A third case study, focused on women's access to legal representation in domestic violence, custody, and eviction proceedings — the areas where representation matters most and is hardest to get.

Theory
Legal representation in protective order, custody, and eviction cases dramatically improves safety and housing outcomes, especially for domestic violence survivors.
Practice
In cities with a right to counsel, represented tenants stay housed at far higher rates than those without a lawyer — sometimes more than double.
The gap
92% of low-income Americans' substantial civil legal problems get no or inadequate help. Unlike criminal cases, there is no federal right to a lawyer in civil court at all.

Mechanism

Unlike criminal defendants, people in civil court have no constitutional right to a free lawyer — a distinction that traces back to the 1963 Supreme Court case that guaranteed counsel only for criminal cases. On top of that structural gap, cost concerns, self-doubt about affording representation, and, for domestic violence survivors specifically, not recognizing a problem as "legal" in nature all compound to keep people out of the system that could protect them. Legal aid organizations funded to help are forced to turn away roughly half of eligible cases due to a lack of resources, a rate that hasn't improved since it was first measured in 2005.

Where the fix is being tried
Right to counsel laws
18 cities and 5 states now guarantee a lawyer in eviction proceedings, though the model rarely extends yet to family law or protective order cases.
Built-in legal presumptions
Laws like California's Family Code 3044 bake protection into the custody decision itself, reducing how much individual advocacy a survivor needs to secure a safe outcome.
