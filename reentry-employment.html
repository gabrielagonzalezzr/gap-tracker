Reentry employment: a gap in what
we know, not just what we do

A fifth case study, and a different kind of gap than the others here — this time between a widely-held, intuitively reasonable theory and what rigorous evaluation has actually been able to confirm.

Theory
Since employment correlates strongly with lower reoffending, getting formerly incarcerated people into jobs quickly should reduce recidivism.
Practice
Most rigorously evaluated employment-focused reentry programs, including a federal multi-site trial, show little to no effect on recidivism.
The gap
Public investment still favors employment-only reentry models despite disappointing trial results, while a more promising combined model remains tested at just one site.
Why this one is different

In the other case studies here, the research clearly identifies a fix that simply isn't adopted widely enough. This one is more honest and more uncertain: researchers themselves say the connection between employment programs and reduced recidivism is "mixed at best," even though the underlying logic is sound and the correlational evidence is strong. That's worth tracking too — not every gap is a failure to implement a known answer. Some are a reminder that the answer isn't fully known yet.

Milwaukee RCT (n=236 high-risk individuals with a history of violence or gang involvement): pre-release "reach-in" services plus post-release subsidized work. Employment and earnings gains were real and persisted through the year. The reincarceration difference shown here was not statistically significant — included honestly, not as proof of an effect.

Mechanism

The economic logic is intuitive: without legitimate income, people are more likely to return to crime. But most post-release-only programs may simply start too late — by the time someone is job-hunting after release, they're often already overwhelmed by housing instability, family strain, and substance use, all at once. The one U.S. trial that combined pre-release preparation with post-release subsidized work showed real employment and earnings gains, suggesting timing and combination of supports may matter more than employment access alone. But that finding comes from a single site, and hasn't yet been replicated at scale.

Where promising signals exist
Reach-in plus wraparound
Combining pre-release preparation with post-release subsidized work and support, tested so far only in Milwaukee at small scale.
Fair-chance hiring policies
Ban-the-box laws and direct employer partnerships, like Chicago transit union apprenticeships, address employer willingness to hire rather than only training the individual.
