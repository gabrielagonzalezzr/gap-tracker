School meals: what the research predicts,
what states actually deliver

The National School Lunch Program is the first case study for tracking the gap between published intervention research and real-world implementation, read through a behavioral-economics lens.

Theory
Removing income-based sorting and paperwork should raise take-up among eligible kids, without changing who is eligible.
Practice
States that made meals free for everyone saw real gains: Vermont, Maine and Colorado grew participation 79–85% since 2020.
The gap
Comparable means-tested states grew only 1–7% in the same period. Only 8 states have made universal meals permanent.

Mechanism

Students who report feeling embarrassed about eating school lunch are significantly less likely to participate, an effect concentrated among lower-income households — the exact group the program is meant to reach. The barrier isn't information about nutrition. It's the social and administrative cost of accessing the benefit: paperwork, visible separate lines, and identity exposure. That reframes the fix from "explain the benefit better" to "remove the friction structurally."

Two structural fixes, same target
Direct certification
Cross-references SNAP and Medicaid data to auto-enroll eligible students, no application required.
Universal free meals
Removes income-based sorting entirely, so no student's meal status is ever visible to peers.
