Youth job training: not all vocational
programs are created equal

A fourth case study, comparing the two dominant models of workforce training for young adults — one proven to work in repeated rigorous trials, one much more heavily funded despite weaker results.

Theory
Employer-linked "sectoral" training, tied to specific in-demand industries with wraparound support, produces large and durable earnings gains.
Practice
Year Up participants earned 30% more annually six years after finishing the program, with no sign of the gains fading.
The gap
The country's largest federally funded youth training program, Job Corps, produces only a 5–10% earnings bump that fades out within a few years.

Mechanism

Traditional broad-based training teaches general skills and hopes participants find a job afterward, without a direct line to specific employers or roles. Sectoral programs work differently: they build curricula around confirmed employer demand in specific industries, add wraparound supports like career coaching and case management, and often place people directly into partner companies. A related randomized study in India found that simply giving trainees clearer information about the jobs actually available to them changed who stayed in the program and who dropped out — the gap between expectation and reality is itself a barrier the traditional model doesn't address.

Two structural fixes, same target
Employer-designed curricula
Programs like Year Up and Per Scholas build training around specific employer partnerships in high-demand sectors like IT and finance.
Wraparound support
Career coaching, case management, and clear information about real job prospects reduce the mismatch-driven dropout traditional programs don't address.
